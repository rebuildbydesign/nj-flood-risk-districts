The Case for Dedicated Funding in District 3
  • District 3 spans Cumberland, Gloucester and Salem counties, which have each experienced 6 to 8 federal disaster declarations from 2011 to 2024.
  • Across the state, 93% of NJ voters want investments to reduce weather damage across party lines and 77% are worried about extreme weather (Fairleigh Dickinson University, 2025).
  • 124 public assets (20%) sit in flood zones today. That rises to 198 (31.9%) by 2050.
  • By 2050, 15 schools, 2 hospitals, 14 fire stations and 5 police stations in the district sit in flood zones.
  • NJ needs a dedicated resilient infrastructure funding source.

Policy Recommendation

S4603 / A5707

New Jersey cannot afford to wait. It needs a $5 billion Clean Water and Safe Communities Bond Act to protect people, properties, tax base and critical public assets.

$1.7 Billion
Flood Prevention & Recovery
  • $400 million for voluntary property buyouts in flood-prone areas
  • $300 million for post-flood recovery
  • $300 million for restoration projects
  • Remaining funds for municipal resilience projects
$1 Billion
Water Infrastructure
  • $400 million for stormwater infrastructure
  • $300 million for wastewater infrastructure
  • $300 million for drinking water infrastructure
$2.3 Billion
Resilience Projects
  • $500 million for grid modernization
  • $600 million for green buildings, including energy efficiency and indoor air quality
  • $450 million for green communities that reduce the impacts of severe weather
  • Remaining funds for other resilience projects
Who ultimately controls spending?
  1. 1The State Office of Emergency Management and the NJ Department of Environmental Protection, with the NJ Infrastructure Bank ranking projects, develop a priority list.
  2. 2Annual spending plans are submitted with budget requests.
  3. 3The Legislature appropriates funds for each specific project.
  4. 4Any transfer of appropriated funds requires Joint Budget Oversight Committee approval.

Funds may be spent directly by the State or awarded as grants to local governments and nonprofit organizations. Full breakdown at rebuildbydesign.org/nj-bond-act.

Methodology & Notes
  • This fact sheet draws from two Rebuild by Design research products: NJ Underwater: Public Infrastructure at Risk (exposure analysis of 18,959 public assets under 2025 and 2050 flood conditions) and the Atlas of Disaster (county-level disaster declarations, 2011 to 2024). District numbers add up the municipalities in each district. Newark and Jersey City are split between districts, so their assets are counted by location. Three assets and one Blue Acres parcel are counted in the town of their street address, so a few town rows differ by one from the city fact sheet; the data workbook (available from info@rebuildbydesign.org) lists each one.
  • Flood zones: An asset counts as at risk if it is within 100 feet of the floodplain. "Today" is the current 1% annual chance (100-year) floodplain, from Rutgers' modified statewide FEMA flood layer. "By 2050" adds three feet of flood elevation, the Rutgers and NJDEP map that aligns with New Jersey's Inland Flood Protection Rule. Water-level flooding only; rainfall runoff and sewer backups are not included.
  • Data Sources: EPA, Fairleigh Dickinson University 2025, FEMA, NJ Legislature, NJ Office of GIS, NJDCA GIS, NJDEP, Rutgers University, Trust for Public Land, US Census 2020, US EIA, USDOT.
